Raw mean r vs. sample-size weighted mean r

The raw mean gives every study one equal vote: rraw = Σri / k. A study of 25 people counts as much as a study of 2,500.

The sample-size weighted mean gives each study a vote in proportion to how many people it observed: r = ΣNiri / ΣNi. This is the same as pooling every participant across studies and asking what the typical correlation is.

Weighting by N is justified because large studies are more precise. The sampling error variance of a single r is about (1 − ρ²)² / (N − 1), so a study with N = 400 has roughly one tenth the error variance of a study with N = 40. Weighting by N closely approximates weighting by inverse error variance, which gives the most accurate estimate of the population mean.

When study size is unrelated to effect size, the two means differ only by chance. When small studies systematically report larger (or smaller) correlations, the raw mean is pulled toward the small studies and the two means come apart. Move the Link between N and r slider to see this.

What the computations mean

Expected sampling error variance (S²e)

The amount of variance in observed correlations we would expect from sampling error alone, even if every study were estimating exactly the same population correlation. It depends only on the mean correlation and the average sample size: the smaller the samples, the more the correlations bounce around by chance. It serves as the baseline against which the observed variance is compared.

Residual variance (S²ρ)

What is left of the observed variance after the expected sampling error variance is subtracted. It estimates how much the population correlations themselves differ from study to study. A residual of zero means sampling error can account for all of the spread; a positive residual suggests real differences between studies, that is, moderators. When the observed variance is smaller than expected by chance, the subtraction gives a negative number, which is treated as zero.

80% credibility interval

Built from the mean correlation and SDρ, the square root of the residual variance. It describes the distribution of population correlations: about 80% of the true correlations in the populations sampled are expected to fall in this range. A narrow interval means the relationship is similar across settings; a wide one means it depends on moderators. With no residual variance, the interval shrinks to a single value.

95% confidence interval

Built from the mean correlation and its standard error, √S²r / k. It describes the precision of the estimated mean: if the meta-analysis were repeated with new samples of studies, 95% of intervals constructed this way would contain the true mean correlation. It narrows as more studies and larger samples are added.

How the two intervals differ. The confidence interval answers “How precisely do we know the average correlation?” The credibility interval answers “How much do the correlations vary across populations?” The confidence interval reflects uncertainty that shrinks with more data, and it would collapse to a point with an infinite number of studies. The credibility interval reflects real variability that does not shrink with more data, because it describes differences among populations rather than error in an estimate. That is why, with the default settings, the confidence interval has width while the credibility interval collapses to a single value: the mean is estimated with some error, but every study is drawn from the same population. Raise the observed variance and the credibility interval widens, often becoming much wider than the confidence interval.

Now, if you haven’t done so already, increase the observed variance value to something beyond its default setting. You can see how the dots in the distribution are now more spread out. Now we have MORE variance than would be expected by chance. This suggests that one or more moderators are present.

Moderators

When the heterogeneity statistics suggest that population correlations differ across studies, the next step is to find out why. Actually, even if there isn’t an exorbitant amount of variance, there may still be moderators. In any case, a moderator is a study characteristic that changes the size of the relationship. How a moderator is tested depends on whether it is categorical or continuous.

Categorical moderators: subgroup analysis

When the moderator has a small number of categories (laboratory vs. field studies, self-report vs. supervisor ratings, published vs. unpublished), studies are sorted by category and a separate meta-analysis is run within each subgroup. The moderator is supported when the subgroup means differ and when the variance within each subgroup is smaller than the variance in the full set of studies, ideally down to what sampling error alone would produce (but don’t hold your breath).

Continuous moderators: meta-regression

When the moderator is continuous (average tenure, mean age, publication year, percentage of women), the effect sizes are regressed on the moderator, with each study weighted by its precision. The slope shows how much the correlation changes per unit of the moderator. Continuous moderators should not be split at the median and analyzed as subgroups; doing so discards information and lowers power.

Moderator analyses in meta-analysis are correlational. Study characteristics tend to travel together (field studies may also use larger samples and different measures), so a moderator can stand in for something else. Each subgroup or regression also needs enough studies for stable estimates.

How to run a subgroup analysis

  1. Code each study on the moderator before looking at the effect sizes.
  2. Meta-analyze each subgroup separately, computing the weighted mean, the observed and sampling error variances, SDρ and the credibility interval within each subgroup (the rows of the table above).
  3. Compare the subgroup means. Qbetween tests whether the subgroup means differ by more than chance; non-overlapping confidence intervals tell a similar story.
  4. Check the heterogeneity within subgroups. If the moderator accounts for the variation, SDρ falls and the percentage of variance due to sampling error rises within each subgroup compared with the overall analysis. Qwithin tests whether heterogeneity remains after the moderator is taken into account.

How meta-regression works

Each study’s correlation is converted to Fisher’s z and regressed on the moderator by weighted least squares. The weights are the inverse of each study’s sampling variance plus τ2res, the variance in true effects left over after the moderator is taken into account (a mixed-effects model). This lets large studies count more while acknowledging that the moderator may not explain everything.

The slope is the change in the effect for a one-unit change in the moderator; its z test and confidence interval show whether the relationship is distinguishable from zero. QM is the corresponding test of the model. R² is the proportion of the between-study variance (τ²) that the moderator explains. QE and τ2res show how much heterogeneity remains; if it is substantial, other moderators are likely at work.

In the Hunter and Schmidt tradition the same question is often addressed by weighted least squares regression of the correlations on the moderator with sample-size weights (Steel & Kammeyer-Mueller, 2002), which gives very similar slopes.

Correction for attenuation

If one or both of the variables whose correlation is being meta-analyzed are measured with error, then there will be differences between the observed and true correlations.

Why measurement error shrinks correlations

Classical test theory treats every observed score as the sum of a true score and random error: X = T + E. Because the errors are random, they are uncorrelated with the true scores and with each other. The observed variance therefore splits into true-score variance and error variance: σ²X = σ²T + σ²E.

Reliability is the share of observed variance that is true-score variance: rxx = σ²T / σ²X. A reliability of .80 means 20% of the variance in the scores is noise.

Error adds variance to each measure but, being random, adds nothing to the covariance between them. The covariance of the observed scores equals the covariance of the true scores, while the standard deviations in the denominator of the correlation are inflated by error. The result is the attenuation formula:

rxy = ρTxTy × √rxx · ryy

The observed correlation is the true correlation multiplied by the square root of the product of the two reliabilities. Since reliabilities are below 1, the observed correlation is always closer to zero than the true one. Rearranging gives Spearman’s correction for attenuation:

ρ = rxy / √rxx · ryy

The correction removes the systematic downward bias but does not add information, so the corrected correlation’s sampling error is enlarged by the same factor: SEc = SEr / √rxx · ryy.

The presence of measurement error affects meta-analysis in two different ways. Every study’s correlation is biased toward zero, so the mean observed r underestimates the mean true correlation. And because reliabilities differ from study to study, they create differences among observed correlations that are not real, adding artifactual variance that can be mistaken for moderators. Hunter and Schmidt’s psychometric meta-analysis corrects each correlation individually when reliabilities are reported, or uses distributions of reliability values when they are not.

Back to meta-analysis: the mean corrected correlation

Because unreliability biases every study’s correlation toward zero, the sample-size weighted mean r from Part 1 estimates the mean observed correlation, not the mean correlation between the constructs. In addition to estimating mean r, then, we can estimate a mean r corrected for unreliability, often written ρ, along with the variance of the corrected correlations.

In principle we could simply correct each study’s correlation for attenuation using its own reliabilities and then meta-analyze the corrected correlations. In practice, not all studies report reliabilities, and studies with single-item or archival measures may have none to report. Dropping those studies would waste data and could bias the sample. As a result, reliability estimates from reliability distributions are typically used: the reliabilities that are reported are compiled into a distribution for each variable, and the mean and variance of that distribution are used to correct the mean and variance of the observed correlations (Hunter & Schmidt’s artifact distribution method).

Reliabilities vary across studies (SD ≈ .06) and are generated for the Part 1 studies. Unreported values appear as dashes in the data table.

Because reliabilities vary across studies, some of the differences in observed correlations are due to reliability variability. This can be estimated with V and paired with the compound attenuation factor A to estimate the variance in corrected correlations. The larger this number is, the more evidence there is that moderators are present.

Study-level data with reliabilities

Artifact distribution method (Hunter & Schmidt, 2004): a = √rxx and b = √ryy are averaged over the studies that report them; the compound attenuation factor is A = a·b. Residual variance is corrected for variation in reliabilities through V, the sum of the squared coefficients of variation of a and b. The comparison estimate corrects only studies reporting both reliabilities, weighted by N·rxx·ryy.

Part 2

Publication bias

Many studies are run, but those that don't produce statistically significant effects have a harder time getting published. This leads to what is called the “file-drawer problem”...the papers that don’t generate significant results end up in the file drawer, and as a result, they aren’t available to be included in meta-analyses. This in turn means that meta-analytic estimates are biased away from zero because the studies that generated effect sizes closer to zero never made it out of the file drawer. Here every statistically significant positive result is published, and a nonsignificant result is published only with the probability you set. The funnel plot shows each study's r against its sample size.

What the funnel shows

Without bias, studies form a symmetric funnel around the true ρ: large studies cluster tightly near it, small studies scatter widely in both directions. The shaded band marks correlations too small to reach p < .05 at each sample size. Small studies can only get out of that band by landing far from zero, so when nonsignificant results stay in the file drawer, the lower part of the funnel loses its left side.

The published literature then overstates the effect. The bias is largest when the true correlation is small and samples are small, because most honest results are nonsignificant. Large studies are nearly always significant and nearly always published, which is why the bias shrinks as mean N grows. Sample-size weighting helps somewhat, since large studies are less distorted, but it cannot recover studies that were never published.

There are many methods for detecting publication bias and for estimating the meta-analytic results that would have been found if such bias were absent. Kepes, Wang, and Cortina (Journal of Business and Psychology, 2023) review these methods, but here are a few of them.

Bias methods applied to the published studies above

Each method below sees only the published studies, as a real meta-analyst would. As explained in Kepes et al. (2023), no single method is trusted on its own. The methods rest on different assumptions, so the goal is to triangulate: if their bias-adjusted estimates converge near the naive mean, the naive mean is robust; if they converge somewhere else, or scatter widely, it is not. Kepes et al. recommend a 7-step process, three of which we illustrate here.

Step 4: Visualize the data

Before running any statistical tests, Kepes et al. recommend plotting the effect sizes, because the shape of the distribution often reveals bias, outliers or heterogeneity that summary numbers hide. The two plots below are the displays they suggest.

Contour-enhanced funnel plot. A funnel plot shows each study’s effect size against its precision (here, its standard error), so you can check whether small and large studies tell the same story. Large, precise studies sit near the top and cluster tightly around the mean; small, imprecise studies sit lower and scatter more widely, so without bias the points form a symmetric inverted funnel. The shading shows which studies would produce statistically significant results at each position. A study plotted in the white area has a nonsignificant result (p > .10); the dark gray band marks results with p between .05 and .10, and the light gray area marks results with p < .05.

A gap is a region of the funnel where studies would be expected but are missing, most often in the lower left, where small studies with small or negative effects would fall. If the gaps fall in the white area, suppression of nonsignificant results is the likely cause of the asymmetry. Trim and fill estimates how many studies are missing and imputes them (open orange circles) as mirror images of the studies on the crowded side. The mean recomputed with these imputed studies (dashed orange line) shows roughly where the mean would be if the funnel were symmetric, and its distance from the naive mean (solid line) indicates how much the missing studies may have distorted the result.

Cumulative meta-analysis by precision. This plot is a running meta-analysis. The studies are first sorted from most to least precise, which in practice means from largest to smallest sample. The top of the chart is a “meta-analysis” of the single most precise study. The second row adds the next most precise study and recomputes the weighted mean, and so on, until the bottom row includes every study and matches the overall mean. The blue line traces the cumulative mean at each step, and the shaded band is its 95% confidence interval, which narrows as studies accumulate.

Reading it. If small studies tell the same story as large ones, the line runs roughly straight down: adding less precise studies only narrows the band. A drift means the mean shifts steadily as smaller studies enter. A rightward drift (toward larger effects) is the typical signature of publication bias, because the small studies that made it into print are disproportionately the ones that happened to find large, significant effects. The size of the drift shows how much the small studies pull the overall mean away from the estimate based on the most precise studies. As with the funnel plot, a drift indicates small-sample bias; publication bias is one cause, but true differences between small and large studies (different samples, measures or settings) can produce the same pattern. The early rows are unstable because they rest on only a few studies, so judge the drift from the overall trend rather than from the first few steps.

Steps 3 and 7: Triangulate and judge the size of the bias

Each dot is a bias-adjusted estimate using a different method (details and citations below). Green band: within 20% of the naive mean (minimal bias). Orange band: 20–40% (moderate). Beyond the orange band: severe. Conventionally, a change of .02 or less counts as minimal regardless of percentage. Dashed line: average of the adjusted estimates.

How the methods work

Trim and fill

A nonparametric method that assumes the funnel would be symmetric without bias. It counts how many studies on the crowded side lack mirror images (here, the L0 estimator), trims those extreme studies, re-estimates the center, and repeats until the count settles. It then fills in mirror-image studies on the sparse side and recomputes the mean. Comparing the naive and adjusted means indicates the severity of the bias. Fixed- and random-effects versions are both shown, as in Kepes et al.’s worked example.

Begg & Mazumdar’s rank correlation and Egger’s test

Both test for funnel asymmetry. Begg and Mazumdar’s test correlates standardized effect sizes with their variances; without bias the two should be unrelated, so a significant correlation suggests bias. Egger’s test regresses each effect divided by its standard error on precision (1/SE). A symmetric funnel gives a line through the origin, so a nonzero intercept means small, imprecise studies differ systematically from large ones. These tests detect asymmetry but do not estimate an adjusted mean.

PET-PEESE

A pair of meta-regressions (Stanley & Doucouliagos, 2014). PET regresses effect sizes on their standard errors and PEESE on their sampling variances, weighting by precision. Each intercept estimates the effect for a hypothetical study with zero standard error, which would not be subject to selection. If the PET intercept is significantly greater than zero, the PEESE estimate is used; otherwise the PET estimate is used.

A priori selection models

Selection (weight-function) models give each effect a weight reflecting how likely it was to be published, based on its p value. Vevea and Woods’s (2005) a priori models fix those weights in advance: significant effects get a weight of 1, and weights fall as p values rise, more steeply in the severe model than in the moderate one. The adjusted mean (and τ²) are then estimated by maximum likelihood. Because they do not rely on the funnel plot, they are not affected by small-sample bias. The severe model can give implausible results and is sometimes omitted.

Methods not shown here

Other selection models, such as Hedges and Vevea’s (1996) estimated weight-function model and Copas and Shi’s model, typically need 100 or more studies. p-curve and p-uniform use only significant effects; p-uniform tends to perform poorly when effects are heterogeneous. The test of excess significance and the mean of the most precise samples are further options.

Rosenthal’s fail-safe N (not recommended)

Fail-safe N estimates how many missing null studies would be needed to make the combined result nonsignificant. Kepes et al. join earlier calls to abandon it: it is crude, it focuses on a nil comparison rather than the size of the effect, and it neither detects bias nor adjusts the estimate. It is shown in the table only for comparison.

Before concluding that publication bias is present (Steps 5 and 6). Funnel-based methods (trim and fill, Begg, Egger, PET-PEESE) actually detect small-sample bias: any systematic difference between small and large studies. Small studies may use different samples (students rather than employees), different or longer measures, or come from different years or outlets, and real moderators or outliers can create the same asymmetry. Heterogeneity should be addressed first, for example by meta-analyzing moderator subgroups and handling outliers, and the bias analyses rerun within those subgroups. Raise the true SD of ρ above to see how heterogeneity affects these results.

All methods use Fisher’s z with sampling variance 1/(N − 3); estimates are converted back to r. Random-effects analyses use the DerSimonian–Laird estimator of τ². Trim and fill uses the L0 estimator and assumes missing studies are on the left. The selection models use Vevea and Woods’s (2005) one-tailed moderate and severe weights and estimate the mean and τ² by maximum likelihood. Bias severity follows the 20% and 40% relative benchmarks, supplemented by a .02 absolute minimum (List et al., 2018), all relative to the random-effects naive mean so that like is compared with like. p-values for t statistics use a normal approximation.
